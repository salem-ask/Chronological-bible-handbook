Archaeology and the Bible

12 discoveries that illuminate the biblical story

Explore archaeological discoveries that shed light on the historical world of the Bible. The twelve objects in this guide are arranged in chronological order, from the Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BC) to the Gallio Inscription (c. AD 52), so you can place each one on the biblical timeline as you study with the Chronological Bible Handbook.

How to read each discovery

Archaeology is a valuable aid to Bible study, but it must be used carefully. For each discovery, this guide keeps three things distinct:

1 · The evidence

What the object itself directly shows: what it says, depicts or names.

2 · The context

The historical background it provides about the world in which the biblical events took place.

3 · The Bible

How it relates to the biblical account: where it agrees, what it adds and where questions remain.

A careful word. Archaeology cannot prove or disprove the whole Bible, and it cannot test spiritual truths such as God's love or the forgiveness of sins. What it can do is show us real places, people and events from the biblical world, and help us read Scripture with a better understanding of its historical setting. Ancient inscriptions also have their own points of view, often royal propaganda, and their readings and dates are sometimes debated. Where scholars disagree, this guide says so. All dates are approximate.

01

c. 1208 BC

The Merneptah Stele

The object
A granite slab about 3 metres high, inscribed in Egyptian hieroglyphs
Discovered
1896, by Flinders Petrie, in the funerary temple of Pharaoh Merneptah at Thebes, Egypt
Where it is today
Egyptian Museum, Cairo

What was discovered

A large royal inscription celebrating Pharaoh Merneptah's victories in his fifth year as king. Most of the text concerns his war against the Libyans; the closing lines list peoples and places in Canaan that he claims to have defeated.

1 What the evidence directly shows

One line names Israel, saying that Israel was laid waste and its seed was no more (a boast of victory). In the hieroglyphs, the name Israel is marked with the sign for a people, while the other names in the list are marked as places or lands. It is generally regarded as the earliest known mention of Israel outside the Bible.

2 The historical context

The stele shows that by about 1208 BC a group called Israel was known in Canaan, important enough to be listed among Pharaoh's enemies. It does not describe how or when Israel came to be there, and the victory it claims is royal propaganda typical of Egyptian inscriptions.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

The Bible describes Israel living in Canaan among other peoples during the time of the Judges. The stele agrees that Israel was present in the land as a people at that general period. Scholars hold different views on the date of the Exodus and the conquest; the stele fits several of these views because it only shows that Israel was already there by about 1208 BC.

Bible references: Joshua 24:31; Judges 1–3

And the children of Israel dwelt among the Canaanites, Hittites, and Amorites, and Perizzites, and Hivites, and Jebusites:

Judges 3:5 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

On the Handbook's biblical timeline, find the conquest of the Promised Land and the beginning of the period of the Judges. The Handbook follows an early date for the Exodus (c. 1445 BC), which places Merneptah's reign within the period of the Judges. Read the stele alongside the Handbook's presentation of the books of Joshua and Judges.

For reflection

Israel appears in history as a people in the land God had promised. How does it encourage you that God's promises to Abraham took shape in real places and among real nations?

02

c. 840 BC

The Mesha Stele (the Moabite Stone)

The object
A black basalt slab about 1.1 metres high, with 34 lines in the Moabite language
Discovered
1868, at Dhiban (biblical Dibon) in present-day Jordan. It was broken into pieces in 1869, but an impression of the text had already been taken, which allowed it to be reconstructed
Where it is today
Musée du Louvre, Paris

What was discovered

An inscription set up by Mesha, king of Moab, describing how he freed Moab from Israelite control and rebuilt towns in his land.

1 What the evidence directly shows

Mesha names Omri, king of Israel, and says that Omri and his son oppressed Moab for many years because Chemosh, the god of Moab, was angry with his land. He describes taking Israelite towns and mentions taking vessels of YHWH, the God of Israel, from Nebo. One damaged line has been read by some scholars as a mention of the “house of David”, but this reading is debated.

2 The historical context

The stele shows that Moab was under Israel's control during the dynasty of Omri, and that Moab later broke free. It also shows how a neighbouring king described his victories in religious terms, crediting his god Chemosh. It is one of the oldest inscriptions that mention the God of Israel by name.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

2 Kings 3 records that Mesha king of Moab paid tribute to the king of Israel and rebelled after Ahab's death. The Bible and the stele describe the same relationship from opposite sides. Each account tells the events from its own point of view: the stele does not mention the campaign of Israel, Judah and Edom against Moab described in 2 Kings 3, and the Bible does not list Mesha's building projects.

Bible references: 1 Kings 16:21–28; 2 Kings 1:1; 3:4–27

But it came to pass, when Ahab was dead, that the king of Moab rebelled against the king of Israel.

2 Kings 3:5 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

In the Handbook's visual table of the kings of Israel and Judah, find Omri, Ahab and Jehoram (Joram). On the biblical timeline, these kings belong to the Kingdom of the North (Israel) after the division of the kingdom, in the time of the prophets Elijah and Elisha.

For reflection

Mesha gave the glory for his victories to Chemosh. When you look back on the good things in your own life, to whom do you give the credit, and why?

03

9th century BC (second half)

The Tel Dan Stele

The object
Three fragments of a basalt victory inscription in Aramaic
Discovered
1993 and 1994, by the excavation team of Avraham Biran at Tel Dan in northern Israel; the pieces had been reused in a later wall
Where it is today
Israel Museum, Jerusalem

What was discovered

Part of a monument set up by a king of Aram-Damascus to celebrate his victories over his enemies. The king's name is not preserved; many scholars attribute the inscription to Hazael.

1 What the evidence directly shows

The text mentions a “king of Israel” and contains the phrase bytdwd, “house of David”. Most scholars understand this as a name for the kingdom of Judah, ruled by David's descendants. The damaged lines also refer to the killing of a king of Israel and a king of the house of David; their names are partly restored by scholars, often as Joram and Ahaziah.

2 The historical context

This is the earliest widely accepted reference to David outside the Bible. Written about 150 years after David's time, it shows that the ruling family of Judah was known by his name to a foreign king.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

The Bible presents David as the founder of a royal line in Judah, and records wars between Israel and Aram in the time of Hazael. 2 Kings 9 records that Jehu killed Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah. If the restored names are correct, the stele's author claims these deaths for himself. Historians discuss how the two accounts relate; one suggestion is that the Aramean king presented events in which Jehu acted as his own victory.

Bible references: 2 Samuel 7:11–16; 2 Kings 8:7–15, 28–29; 9:14–29

And he went with Joram the son of Ahab to the war against Hazael king of Syria in Ramoth-gilead; and the Syrians wounded Joram.

2 Kings 8:28 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

Use the Handbook's visual table of the kings of Israel and Judah to locate Joram of Israel and Ahaziah of Judah, and the timeline to see the divided kingdom: Israel in the North and Judah in the South, where David's descendants reigned.

For reflection

A foreign king called Judah “the house of David”. What does God's promise of a lasting house for David (2 Samuel 7) mean for you as you read the rest of the Bible?

04

c. 825 BC (records events of 841 BC)

The Black Obelisk of Shalmaneser III

The object
A black limestone pillar about 2 metres high, with five rows of carved panels and an Assyrian inscription
Discovered
1846, by Austen Henry Layard, at Nimrud (ancient Kalhu) in present-day Iraq
Where it is today
British Museum, London

What was discovered

A monument of the Assyrian king Shalmaneser III recording his military campaigns and the tribute he received from several rulers.

1 What the evidence directly shows

One panel shows a man bowing to the ground before the Assyrian king, followed by servants carrying gifts. The caption calls it the tribute of Jehu, son of Omri. “Son of Omri” was the Assyrian way of referring to the king of Israel, the land of the house of Omri; Jehu was not Omri's descendant. Scholars discuss whether the kneeling figure is Jehu himself or his envoy.

2 The historical context

The obelisk shows that the kingdom of Israel paid tribute to Assyria in Jehu's time (841 BC), as Assyria's power was growing in the region. It is one of the earliest surviving pictures of an Israelite king or his representative.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

The Bible describes Jehu as king of Israel after Joram and records that, in his days, Hazael of Aram attacked Israel. It does not mention Jehu's tribute to Assyria. The obelisk adds a detail from the Assyrian side; some historians suggest that Jehu sought Assyria's favour while Israel was under pressure from Aram.

Bible references: 2 Kings 9–10

In those days the LORD began to cut Israel short: and Hazael smote them in all the coasts of Israel;

2 Kings 10:32 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

Find Jehu in the Handbook's visual table of the kings of Israel and Judah, and read the Handbook's presentation of the books of Kings to see where his reign fits in the history of the Northern Kingdom.

For reflection

Jehu bowed before a powerful earthly king. In what situations are you tempted to look for security in human power rather than in God?

05

c. 700 BC

The Siloam Inscription and Hezekiah's Tunnel

The object
A Hebrew inscription of six lines, cut into the wall of a water tunnel about 533 metres long
Discovered
The tunnel was known before modern times; the inscription was found inside it in 1880, near its southern end
Where it is today
Inscription: Istanbul Archaeology Museums. The tunnel can still be walked through in Jerusalem

What was discovered

A tunnel cut through solid rock to bring water from the Gihon Spring, outside the old city wall of Jerusalem, to a pool inside the city.

1 What the evidence directly shows

The inscription describes the moment when two teams of workers, digging from opposite ends, heard each other's voices and broke through to meet. It says that the water then flowed from the spring to the pool for 1,200 cubits. It does not name the king who ordered the work.

2 The historical context

The tunnel shows the engineering skill of Jerusalem's builders and the importance of securing the city's water supply in a time of war. Most scholars date the inscription and the tunnel to the reign of Hezekiah, around 700 BC, mainly from the style of the Hebrew script and the biblical description; a few have proposed other dates.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

The Bible records that Hezekiah made a pool and a conduit and brought water into the city, and that he stopped the upper watercourse of Gihon and brought it down to the west side of the city of David. The tunnel matches this description well, and is widely identified with Hezekiah's work.

Bible references: 2 Kings 20:20; 2 Chronicles 32:2–4, 30; Isaiah 22:9–11

And the rest of the acts of Hezekiah, and all his might, and how he made a pool, and a conduit, and brought water into the city, are they not written in the book of the chronicles of the kings of Judah?

2 Kings 20:20 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

Find Hezekiah in the Handbook's visual table of the kings of Israel and Judah. On the biblical timeline, his reign follows the fall of Samaria (722 BC), in the time of the prophets Isaiah and Micah.

For reflection

Hezekiah prepared wisely for danger and also prayed to the LORD (2 Kings 19). How do you hold together careful planning and trust in God?

06

c. 691–689 BC (records the campaign of 701 BC)

Sennacherib's Prism

The object
Six-sided clay prisms inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform
Discovered
Several copies of Sennacherib's annals are known. The Taylor Prism, from Nineveh, was obtained by Colonel Robert Taylor in 1830
Where it is today
Taylor Prism: British Museum, London. Other copies: Oriental Institute, Chicago, and Israel Museum, Jerusalem

What was discovered

The official annals of the Assyrian king Sennacherib, describing his military campaigns, including his third campaign in 701 BC against the Levant and Judah.

1 What the evidence directly shows

Sennacherib names Hezekiah of Judah. He claims to have besieged and captured 46 of Hezekiah's fortified cities and to have shut Hezekiah up in Jerusalem “like a bird in a cage”. He lists the tribute Hezekiah sent, including 30 talents of gold and 800 talents of silver. He does not claim to have captured Jerusalem.

2 The historical context

The prism shows, from the Assyrian side, that a major Assyrian invasion of Judah took place in 701 BC. Its account is written to glorify the Assyrian king; it says nothing of why the campaign ended without the capture of Jerusalem.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

The Bible also describes Sennacherib's invasion, the capture of Judah's fortified cities and Hezekiah's tribute: thirty talents of gold (the same figure as the prism) and three hundred talents of silver (the prism gives a higher figure). It then records that Jerusalem was delivered and that Sennacherib returned to Nineveh. The two accounts agree on the main events and differ in emphasis and in some details.

Bible references: 2 Kings 18:13–19:37; 2 Chronicles 32:1–23; Isaiah 36–37

Now in the fourteenth year of king Hezekiah did Sennacherib king of Assyria come up against all the fenced cities of Judah, and took them.

2 Kings 18:13 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

Read this together with the Handbook's presentation of 2 Kings and Isaiah, and find Hezekiah in the visual table of the kings of Israel and Judah.

For reflection

Hezekiah took Sennacherib's threatening letter and spread it before the LORD (2 Kings 19:14). What “letter” or worry would you like to bring before God today?

07

late 7th or early 6th century BC

The Ketef Hinnom Silver Scrolls

The object
Two tiny rolled sheets of silver, worn as amulets, inscribed in ancient Hebrew script
Discovered
1979, by Gabriel Barkay's team, in a burial cave at Ketef Hinnom, on the slope of the Hinnom Valley in Jerusalem
Where it is today
Israel Museum, Jerusalem

What was discovered

Two small silver scrolls, only a few centimetres long. They were so fragile that it took several years to unroll them safely and read the faint text.

1 What the evidence directly shows

Both scrolls contain a blessing very close to the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24–26, including the divine name YHWH. Most scholars date them to the late 7th or early 6th century BC, the last years of the kingdom of Judah; a few have argued for a later date.

2 The historical context

These are the oldest known objects bearing words that are also found in the Bible, older than the Dead Sea Scrolls by several centuries. They show that this blessing was known and treasured by people in Jerusalem before the Babylonian exile.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

The scrolls contain a form of the blessing that the LORD gave to Aaron and his sons to speak over Israel. They do not by themselves tell us when the book of Numbers was written, but they show that the words of this blessing were in use in Judah at this time.

Bible references: Numbers 6:22–27

The LORD bless thee, and keep thee: The LORD make his face shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee: The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace.

Numbers 6:24–26 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

Read the Handbook's presentation of the book of Numbers. Then, on the biblical timeline, look at the final years of the kingdom of Judah and the ministry of Jeremiah, the period from which these scrolls most probably come.

For reflection

Someone in ancient Jerusalem carried these words of blessing with them. Which words of Scripture do you carry with you, and how do they shape your days?

08

records events of 605–594 BC, including March 597 BC

The Babylonian Chronicle of Nebuchadnezzar

The object
A small clay tablet inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform
Discovered
Part of a series of Babylonian chronicle tablets held in the British Museum; this tablet was first published in 1956
Where it is today
British Museum, London

What was discovered

A brief year-by-year record of the first years of Nebuchadnezzar II, king of Babylon, including the battle of Carchemish (605 BC) and his campaigns in the west.

1 What the evidence directly shows

For Nebuchadnezzar's seventh year, the chronicle records that he besieged “the city of Judah”, captured it on the second day of the month Adar (March 597 BC), seized its king, appointed a king of his own choice, and took heavy tribute to Babylon.

2 The historical context

This entry gives one of the most precise dates in the history of ancient Judah. It shows Judah caught between the great powers of Egypt and Babylon in the years before the destruction of Jerusalem.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

2 Kings 24 records that Nebuchadnezzar besieged Jerusalem, took King Jehoiachin to Babylon with treasures and many people, and made Mattaniah, renamed Zedekiah, king in his place. The chronicle does not give the kings' names but describes the same events. It dates them to Nebuchadnezzar's seventh year, while 2 Kings 24:12 says the eighth year; scholars generally explain this by different ways of counting a king's years.

Bible references: 2 Kings 24:8–17; 2 Chronicles 36:9–10; Jeremiah 46:2

And Jehoiachin the king of Judah went out to the king of Babylon, he, and his mother, and his servants, and his princes, and his officers: and the king of Babylon took him in the eighth year of his reign.

2 Kings 24:12 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

On the Handbook's biblical timeline, find the final years of the kingdom of Judah, King Zedekiah (598–587 BC) and the deportation of the inhabitants of Judah to Babylon. Ezekiel and Daniel, also on the timeline, were among those taken into exile in this period.

For reflection

The prophets had warned Judah for many years before these events. How do you respond when God's Word calls you to change direction?

09

c. 588–586 BC

The Lachish Letters

The object
Pieces of broken pottery (ostraca) with letters written in ink in ancient Hebrew
Discovered
1935 (with a few more in 1938), by the expedition of James Leslie Starkey at Tell ed-Duweir, ancient Lachish, in a room near the city gate
Where it is today
British Museum, London, and Israel Museum, Jerusalem

What was discovered

Military letters, mostly sent by an officer named Hoshayahu to Yaush, apparently the commander at Lachish, during the Babylonian invasion of Judah.

1 What the evidence directly shows

In Letter 4, the writer says that he and his men are watching for the fire signals of Lachish, because they cannot see Azekah. Other letters mention a prophet, whose name is not preserved, and reflect the anxious situation of the officers.

2 The historical context

These letters give a rare first-hand view of Judah's last days before the fall of Jerusalem, written by people living through the Babylonian attack. Many historians understand the remark about Azekah to mean that Azekah had already fallen or could no longer send signals.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

Jeremiah 34:7 says that when the Babylonian army fought against Jerusalem, Lachish and Azekah were the last fortified cities of Judah still standing. The letters fit this situation closely.

Bible references: Jeremiah 34:1–7; 2 Kings 25:1–2

When the king of Babylon’s army fought against Jerusalem, and against all the cities of Judah that were left, against Lachish, and against Azekah: for these defenced cities remained of the cities of Judah.

Jeremiah 34:7 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

On the Handbook's biblical timeline, find the ministry of Jeremiah (626–586 BC), King Zedekiah, and the fall of Jerusalem (587 or 586 BC).

For reflection

The officers at Lachish watched anxiously for signals in a time of crisis. Where do you look for guidance and hope when everything around you is uncertain?

10

c. 539–538 BC

The Cyrus Cylinder

The object
A barrel-shaped clay cylinder, about 23 centimetres long, inscribed in Akkadian cuneiform
Discovered
1879, by Hormuzd Rassam, in the ruins of Babylon, in present-day Iraq
Where it is today
British Museum, London

What was discovered

A royal inscription written after Cyrus the Great of Persia took Babylon in 539 BC, presenting him as a just ruler chosen by Marduk, the god of Babylon.

1 What the evidence directly shows

Cyrus states that he returned the images of gods to their sanctuaries in several cities and gathered their former inhabitants and returned them to their homes. The cities named are in Mesopotamia and western Iran. The cylinder does not mention Judah, Jerusalem or the Jews.

2 The historical context

The cylinder shows the general policy of Cyrus: allowing displaced peoples to return home and restore their places of worship, partly to win their loyalty. It is written in the language of Babylonian royal propaganda.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

The Bible records that Cyrus issued a decree allowing the Jews to return to Jerusalem and rebuild the house of the LORD. The cylinder does not refer to this decree, but it shows that such a policy was consistent with the way Cyrus governed. Isaiah names Cyrus as the one through whom the LORD would bring about the rebuilding of Jerusalem and the temple.

Bible references: 2 Chronicles 36:22–23; Ezra 1:1–4; 6:3–5; Isaiah 44:28–45:1

Thus saith Cyrus king of Persia, The LORD God of heaven hath given me all the kingdoms of the earth; and he hath charged me to build him an house at Jerusalem, which is in Judah.

Ezra 1:2 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

On the Handbook's biblical timeline, find the beginning of Persian rule (539 BC), when Cyrus, king of Persia, ordered the return of the Jews (538 BC), followed by the restoration period with Haggai and Zechariah.

For reflection

God worked even through a foreign king to bring His people home. Where have you seen God at work through unexpected people or circumstances?

11

AD 26–36

The Pilate Stone

The object
A limestone block with a partly preserved Latin inscription
Discovered
1961, by Italian archaeologists led by Antonio Frova, in the Roman theatre at Caesarea Maritima, Israel, where the stone had been reused in a later building phase
Where it is today
Israel Museum, Jerusalem (a replica stands at Caesarea)

What was discovered

A dedication stone from a building in honour of the emperor Tiberius (a “Tiberieum”) at Caesarea, the Roman administrative centre of Judea.

1 What the evidence directly shows

The inscription names Pontius Pilate and gives his title as prefect of Judea. It is the only known stone inscription from Pilate's own time that names him.

2 The historical context

The stone shows that Pilate governed Judea under the emperor Tiberius and gives his official title. Pilate is also mentioned by the Jewish writers Philo and Josephus and by the Roman historian Tacitus.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

All four Gospels name Pontius Pilate as the Roman governor before whom Jesus was tried and who handed Him over to be crucified. Luke dates the beginning of John the Baptist's ministry to the fifteenth year of Tiberius, when Pilate was governor of Judea. The stone places Pilate in the same office and the same period.

Bible references: Matthew 27:1–26; Mark 15:1–15; Luke 3:1; 23:1–25; John 18:28–19:16

Now in the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Cæsar, Pontius Pilate being governor of Judæa, and Herod being tetrarch of Galilee, …

Luke 3:1 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

On the Handbook's biblical timeline, find the life of Jesus: His baptism and the beginning of His public ministry, and His death and resurrection (AD 30), which took place while Pilate governed Judea.

For reflection

Pilate asked, “What is truth?” (John 18:38) while standing before Jesus. How would you answer that question today?

12

c. AD 52

The Gallio Inscription (Delphi Inscription)

The object
Several fragments of a stone inscription in Greek
Discovered
Fragments found at Delphi, Greece, during French excavations; first published in 1905, with further fragments identified later
Where it is today
Delphi Archaeological Museum, Greece

What was discovered

A copy of a letter from the Roman emperor Claudius to the city of Delphi, set up publicly at the sanctuary of Apollo.

1 What the evidence directly shows

The emperor refers to Gallio as his friend and proconsul (governor) of the province of Achaia. The letter is dated by Claudius' 26th acclamation as emperor, which places it in AD 52, most probably between January and August.

2 The historical context

Roman proconsuls of this kind usually served for one year. Using this inscription, historians date Gallio's term in Achaia to about AD 51–52 (some prefer AD 50–51). Gallio was the brother of the philosopher Seneca.

3 How it relates to the biblical account

Acts 18 records that while Paul was in Corinth, the capital of Achaia, he was brought before Gallio's judgment seat, and Gallio refused to hear the case. Because Gallio's term can be dated, this inscription is one of the key fixed points for dating Paul's stay in Corinth and the chronology of his journeys and letters.

Bible references: Acts 18:1–17

And when Gallio was the deputy of Achaia, the Jews made insurrection with one accord against Paul, and brought him to the judgment seat,

Acts 18:12 (KJV)

In the Chronological Bible Handbook

In the Handbook, look at the visual presentation of Paul's three missionary journeys and, on the biblical timeline, the second missionary journey of Paul (c. AD 50–52), during which Paul stayed in Corinth.

For reflection

Paul kept preaching in Corinth after the Lord told him, “Be not afraid, but speak” (Acts 18:9). Where might God be calling you to speak about your faith?

Using this guide

For personal study and for groups

  • Read the Bible passage first. Each discovery lists the related Bible references. Read them in your Bible before reading the archaeological notes.
  • Find the period in the Handbook. Use the biblical timeline and the visual tables of the Chronological Bible Handbook to see where each discovery fits in the story.
  • Keep the three levels distinct. Ask: What does the object show? What does it tell us about the period? How does it relate to the biblical text?
  • End with the reflection question. In a group, give each person time to answer, and close in prayer.

These discoveries do not replace faith, and faith does not depend on the next discovery from the ground. Yet they remind us that the Bible speaks of real people and real events, in a real world where God was at work.
